\chapter{Method}\label{chapter:method}
 
\section{Introduction}
 
A spatial prior asserts that risk varies smoothly across space. Temperature does. Heat vulnerability does not: a tower block without air conditioning abuts detached housing with central air, and the two meet the same heat on the same day \needcite{air conditioning prevalence by housing type --- Canadian source}. The contrast is sharpest at the temperature extremes, which is where the estimand sits, so smoothing is worst exactly where the estimate matters most. This design therefore carries no geographic term. What each area borrows, it borrows through what is measured about it, and areas that share a boundary are neighbours in the data only if they also resemble each other.
 
Carrying no geographic term has a price. A design that smooths across neighbours leaves residual spatial pattern in its own output, so structure the model missed can be read off the map it produced. This one cannot: whatever the indicators fail to capture is absorbed into noise and never appears as pattern. The design therefore cannot audit its own coverage, and the audit has to be borrowed---a fit on the same data that does carry a spatial term, whose residual structure is evidence precisely because it comes from a model this one did not build, which Chapter~\ref{chapter:validation} takes up.
 
Adjacency is not the only way to let risk vary. The curve can be bent directly by a named characteristic---deprivation, say---fitted as an interaction, and one modifier at a time is tractable~\citep{rutten2026effectmod}. Vulnerability does not arrive one at a time. Age structure, income, housing quality and dwelling density move together across a city, and predictors that move together are separated only by the variation where they do not, so their individual coefficients stay unstable however long the model runs. This is an identification problem, not a computational one; more data does not fix it. Asking what income does with housing held fixed is asking about variation the city does not contain, and dimension reduction works by not asking: the correlated bundle is rotated into a few components, and risk is estimated along those.

\section{The first-stage model}
 
The first stage fits a temperature--mortality association separately within each census metropolitan area and each of four age groups, using the case time series design of Gasparrini~\citep{gasparrini2021}. The parent model is a conditional Poisson regression of the daily death count on temperature; we use it in the form given by Gasparrini et al.~\citep{gasparrini2022smallarea}, specialized to the CMA as the pooling unit:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq_firststage}
\log E(Y_{i,t}) = \xi_{i(k)} + \mathrm{cb}(T_{i,t}, \ell;\, \theta_{c,a}) + s(t;\, \gamma_{c,a}) + I(w_{t};\, \varphi_{c,a}),
\end{equation}
%
where
\begin{itemize}
  \item $Y_{i,t}$ is the death count in dissemination area $i$ on day $t$, assumed Poisson distributed;
  \item $\xi_{i(k)}$ is the stratum intercept, defined for each dissemination area $i$ within stratum $k$, where strata index month-in-year subsets of the study period;
  \item $\mathrm{cb}(T_{i,t}, \ell;\, \theta_{c,a})$ is the cross-basis of temperature $T_{i,t}$ over lag $\ell$, with coefficients $\theta_{c,a}$ specific to census metropolitan area $c$ and age group $a$;
  \item $s(t;\, \gamma_{c,a})$ is a natural spline of time with one degree of freedom per year, carrying long-term trend;
  \item $I(w_{t};\, \varphi_{c,a})$ are day-of-week indicators.
\end{itemize}
 
The stratum intercepts $\xi_{i(k)}$ compare each day only against other days in the same neighbourhood, season, and year, so that long-term trend, seasonality, and every fixed neighbourhood feature are held constant by construction. The conditional Poisson estimator of Armstrong et al.~\citep{armstrong2014} conditions them out, leaving only $\theta_{c,a}$, $\gamma_{c,a}$, and $\varphi_{c,a}$ to estimate.
 
The design works with aggregate data, and one would expect the ecological fallacy to be its central threat. The counts are deaths per neighbourhood per day, never deaths of individuals, and an association read from group counts need not hold for the people inside the group. The threat is real, and the design meets it not by escaping aggregation but by where it identifies the association. The temperature effect is estimated within a place, across its days. The quantity estimated is the conditional probability that a death in a neighbourhood fell on a hotter day rather than a cooler one. Aggregated across strata, that is the answer to whether heat raised the risk of death in that place. The inference is thus about days in a neighbourhood, and it never crosses from the neighbourhood to the person, because it was never a comparison between neighbourhoods to begin with.
 
We fit within the metropolitan area, not the dissemination area, because the daily death count in a single dissemination area is too low to identify the association on its own. The cross-basis is specified exactly as in the source~\citep{gasparrini2022smallarea}: a quadratic B-spline in temperature with internal knots at the 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of each city's temperature distribution, and a natural cubic spline over lags 0 to 21 days with knots at equally spaced points on the log scale of the lag.
 
The cross-basis is one object built from two. The quadratic B-spline in temperature spans a five-dimensional space, and the natural cubic spline over the lag window spans another. Their tensor product is the cross-basis: every temperature basis function multiplied by every lag basis function, so that a single coefficient reads the risk of a given temperature at a given lag. Five temperature terms and five lag terms give $5 \times 5 = 25$ coefficients, and these are the $\theta_{c,a}$ of Equation~\ref{eq_firststage}. The temperature basis is built without an intercept. An intercept there would make one temperature function constant, its product with the lag basis would repeat the lag basis exactly, and the cross-basis would be rank-deficient~\citep{gasparrini2014dlnm}.
 
\section{Reduction}
 
The first stage returns one fitted association per metropolitan area and age group, carried in the cross-basis coefficients $\theta_{c,a}$ with covariance $\Sigma_{c,a}$. Before pooling, we reduce each association to its overall cumulative form $\theta^{*}_{c,a}$, summing the effect across the lag window into a single curve~\citep{gasparrini2013reduce}.
 
The pooling cannot proceed without the reduction. The units entering the second stage are metropolitan areas by age groups, 41 by 4, 164 in all. An unstructured between-unit covariance $\Psi$ grows with the number of curve coefficients: at the full cross-basis of 25 it carries $25 \cdot 26 / 2 = 325$ free parameters against those 164 units, a ratio below one to two that no sample of this size could support. Reducing each association to its overall-cumulative curve takes the coefficients from 25 to 5 and $\Psi$ from 325 parameters to 15, which moves the ratio to about 11 units per parameter. It clears the conventional floor of 10, and it is thin. The number of units is fixed by the country and cannot be raised; the reduction is the only lever, and it has been pulled. The reduction is a linear map applied to each fitted association, exact on both the curve and its covariance, so the uncertainty from the first stage is carried into the pooling rather than approximated~\citep{gasparrini2013reduce}.
 
\section{The second-stage model}
 
We pool the reduced curves with a multivariate mixed-effects meta-regression~\citep{sera2019}:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq_secondstage}
\theta^{*}_{c,a} = X_{c,a}\,\beta + Z_{c,a}\,b_{c,a} + \varepsilon_{c,a},
\end{equation}
%
where
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\theta^{*}_{c,a}$ is the reduced cumulative curve for metropolitan area $c$ and age group $a$;
  \item $X_{c,a}$ is the design matrix of fixed-effects meta-predictors, holding age indicators and the composite vulnerability indicators;
  \item $\beta$ is the vector of fixed-effects coefficients;
  \item $Z_{c,a}\,b_{c,a}$ are between-area random effects, retained only if a multivariate Cochran's $Q$ test calls for them~\citep{gasparrini2013reduce, sera2019};
  \item $\varepsilon_{c,a}$ is the sampling error carried forward from the first stage, with (co)variance $V^{*}_{c,a}$.
\end{itemize}
 
The meta-predictors in $X_{c,a}$ are not the raw neighbourhood variables but a small number of composite indicators built from them by principal component analysis, following the source~\citep{gasparrini2022smallarea}. We construct these in Chapter~\ref{chapter:vulnerability}.
 
Three checks confirm the pooled model is internally sound before any neighbourhood is read from it. The first is convergence: the estimate is taken only once the restricted likelihood has converged. The second is that $\hat{\Psi}$ is a covariance, confirmed by Cholesky decomposition, since a matrix that fails this test describes no distribution and cannot be sampled from downstream. The third is residual heterogeneity: after the vulnerability indicators and age have explained what they can, the $I^{2}$ statistic measures how much variation between units remains.
 
Choosing the number of components needs one point of care. Each candidate count is a different set of fixed-effects meta-predictors, and the Akaike information criterion compares fixed-effects specifications only when the models are fitted by maximum likelihood. Under restricted maximum likelihood the comparison is not licensed, because the restricted likelihood conditions on the fixed effects it is meant to compare~\citep{sera2019}. We therefore fit the candidate models by maximum likelihood, select the component count that minimises their AIC, and refit the chosen model by restricted maximum likelihood to estimate its variance components.
 
\section{Downscaling to the neighbourhood}
 
The pooled model is fitted at the metropolitan scale but written in terms of neighbourhood characteristics. For each dissemination area $d$ and age group $a$ we evaluate the fixed-effect part of the meta-regression at that area's own composite indicators,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}^{*}_{d,a} = X_{d,a}\,\hat{\beta},
\end{equation}
and read the result as the area's temperature--mortality curve --- the prediction given by equation 5 of \citet{sera2019}. Nothing is fitted at the dissemination area; the area supplies its indicators and receives a curve.
 
A mixed model can do more than evaluate the population relationship at a unit's covariates. It can add back the unit's departure from that relationship---the best linear unbiased predictor,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}^{*}_{d,a} = X_{d,a}\,\hat{\beta}
    + Z_{d,a}\,\hat{\Psi}\,Z_{d,a}^{\top}\,\hat{\Sigma}_{d,a}^{-1}
      \!\left(\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{d,a} - X_{d,a}\,\hat{\beta}\right),
\end{equation}
the population prediction corrected by the unit's own residual, weighted by how much that residual can be trusted~\citep{sera2019}. But the correction needs the residual, and the residual needs a curve $\tilde{\theta}^{*}_{d,a}$ fitted at the dissemination area itself. The design has no such curve: a dissemination area carries too few deaths to fit the first stage, which is the whole reason the estimate is borrowed from the metropolitan scale to begin with. With nothing to correct against, the downscale is the fixed-effect prediction alone.
 
Reading the downscale as the fixed-effect prediction fixes the map's character. With no residual to trust, every dissemination area is pulled fully onto the population relationship, and its curve differs from its neighbour's only as their indicators differ. The surface is therefore smoother than the truth it estimates. Two areas that differ sharply in risk but only mildly in measured vulnerability are drawn together on the map, and the spread between them is understated~\citep[expr.~7.7]{VM_TODO}. This is shrinkage, and it is a property of the estimator.
 
From each predicted curve we take the minimum-mortality temperature as the reference, and summarize the danger of heat as the relative risk at a high temperature percentile against that reference. Doing this for every dissemination area produces the national surface reported in Chapter~\ref{chapter:nationalmap}.

\section{Discussion}
 
The downscale assumes that the relationship between neighbourhood characteristics and risk, estimated at the metropolitan scale, holds at the finer scale of the dissemination area. We test that assumption in Chapter~\ref{chapter:validation}.
 
Two limits sit on the downscaled curve, and they are not the same kind. The first is the shrinkage just described: the surface is smoother than the truth, and the validation in Chapter~\ref{chapter:validation} can measure how much, since expression~(7.7) says what to expect. The second was fixed before the pooling ever ran. Reducing each association to its cumulative curve summed the effect across the lag window into a single number, and the timing inside that window did not survive the sum. The downscaled curve carries how much heat raises risk; it cannot carry whether the risk falls on the same day in one neighbourhood and three days later in another. No validation recovers this, of course---the information was gone before the second stage began.
 
In the next chapter we apply the design across Canada's major cities and present the first neighbourhood-level map of heat-related mortality risk for the country.
